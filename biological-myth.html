The Biological Myth

1959–1963  ·  Life Magazine & the Lovelace Tests

Before Congress invented a policy barrier, NASA's own physicians invented a scientific one. The assumption was simple and stated plainly: women were physically unfit for the demands of spaceflight. The Lovelace tests of 1959–1961 demolished that assumption entirely. What happened next reveals how power, not evidence, determines who gets to make history.

LIFE
August 29, 1960  ·  Page 72

PHOTOGRAPHIC ESSAY

"A Lady Proves She's Fit for Space Flight"

Exclusive Pictures Depict Jerrie Cobb's Courage and Aptitude in a Hitherto Secret Program for Women

"After a series of exhaustive and exhausting medical tests, 75 in all, during which she complained less than the Mercury men had, Jerrie Cobb easily passed the rigid requirements laid down for astronauts-in-training."
— Life Magazine, August 29, 1960, p. 72

This single sentence is the evidentiary foundation of the entire Mercury 13 story. Life — the most widely read magazine in America — declared, on the record, that a woman had passed every test the Mercury 7 had taken, and had done so with fewer complaints. The biological case against women astronauts had been made and refuted in the same moment. What remained was purely a question of will.

In Their Own Words: Wally Funk

NASA's own oral history program recorded Wally Funk's account of the Lovelace tests in 1999. Her testimony is the most detailed first-person record of what the women actually endured.

Sensory Deprivation Test
"I had not a shadow of a doubt. I was their subject. They could do anything with me that they wanted to do."
— Wally Funk, NASA Oral History, July 18, 1999

Funk set the record in the isolation tank at 10 hours and 35 minutes. The Mercury 7 men were tested in a lit room. Funk floated in total darkness with all five senses removed.

The Numbers Argument
"159 men were selected to test for Mercury and 7 were chosen. 25 women were selected and 13 passed. Do we have a little bit of information here on how well women do things? A dog did it. A monkey did it. Man did it. Woman can do it."
— Wally Funk, NASA Oral History, July 18, 1999
The G-Force Test
"Civilian women were only permitted 3 Gs and were not allowed G suits."
— Wally Funk, NASA Oral History, July 18, 1999

Funk improvised, requesting "her worst Merry Widow and girdle" from her mother, modified it into a makeshift G suit, and wore it under her flight suit without telling testers — a detail that didn't emerge publicly for 25 years.

Full transcript available: NASA Oral History — Wally Funk, July 18, 1999 (PDF)

HISTORICAL ANALYSIS

The biological myth served a specific institutional purpose: it placed the burden of proof on the women rather than on NASA. Once Cobb and twelve others demolished that proof, the argument shifted — not to accepting women, but to inventing a new barrier. The Life article made this maneuver visible to the entire country. The question was no longer whether women could do it. The question became whether they would be allowed to.
